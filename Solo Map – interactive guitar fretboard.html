How to use the map

  1. Set the key. This is the root of the song, usually the chord the progression starts and ends on. If the song is in A minor, choose A.
  2. Choose a scale that matches the mood. Minor pentatonic is the safe default for rock and blues. Major pentatonic suits country and bright pop. The full major and natural minor scales add the notes pentatonic leaves out. Use Dorian for minor with a lift, Mixolydian for major with a bluesy, rock edge.
  3. Pick one position. “All positions” shows the whole neck, which is useful for seeing the pattern but too much to play from. Choose a single position and the dots from the other positions fade into the background. Stay in one position until you can play it without looking, then move to the next one up.
  4. Choose a target chord. The teal dots are the three notes of that chord. When the band changes chords, switch this setting and aim your phrases at the teal dots, especially at the start of the bar. This is what makes a solo sound like it belongs to the song.
  5. Switch between Notes and Intervals. Notes shows letter names. Intervals shows each dot’s distance from the root (1, b3, 5…), which is how the pattern stays the same in every key.
  6. Listen. Click a dot to hear it, or press Play scale to hear the visible notes climb one octave from the root.

A simple practice routine

  • Play the position slowly, up and down, saying each note name or interval aloud.
  • Play only the root notes, then only the chord tones, so you can find them without hunting.
  • Make up a three-note phrase and move it to every string in the position.
  • Put on a backing track in the same key. Start each phrase on a chord tone and end it on one.
  • Change the key and play the same shape. The pattern moves with the root, so the fingering stays the same.

The fretboard shows frets 0 to 15. Every pattern repeats an octave higher at fret 12, so a position near the nut also appears around the 12th fret.

The theory behind it

Notes, semitones and intervals

Western music has 12 notes, and each fret is one semitone (half step) apart. After 12 frets you are back at the same note an octave higher. An interval is the distance from the root counted in semitones: 3 semitones is a minor third (b3), 4 is a major third (3), 7 is a perfect fifth (5), and so on. Every scale is just a list of intervals from a root, so once you know the formula you can build the scale in any key.

Why pentatonic scales are so forgiving

Pentatonic scales leave out the two notes that most often clash with chords: the 4th and 7th of the major scale, or the 2nd and b6 of the minor. With fewer risky notes, almost anything you play sounds acceptable, and that is why beginners start there.

Chord tones are your targets

A triad is the root, third and fifth of a chord. These are the strongest notes against that chord. The third tells the listener whether the chord is major or minor, so landing on it makes your line follow the harmony. Scale notes that are not chord tones sound like tension, which is useful on weak beats and as passing notes. Resolve them to a chord tone for the satisfying “arrival”.

Positions and how they connect

The neck contains the same scale in several overlapping shapes. A pentatonic scale has five positions (the “boxes”), and a seven-note scale has seven. Each one here starts from one scale note on the low E string. They link end to end: the top of one position is the bottom of the next, so sliding up or down a string takes you between them. Learning them one at a time, and then connecting two neighbours, opens up the whole neck.

The blue note

The b5 in the blues scale sits a semitone away from both the 4th and the 5th. Held for a long time it sounds wrong, but as a quick passing note, a slide or half of a bend, it gives the blues its tension. Play 4, b5, 5 in a row and you hear it immediately.

Minor over major, and the other way round

Blues players often use a minor pentatonic over a major (dominant 7th) chord. The b3 against the chord’s major 3rd creates the characteristic bluesy friction. Rock players commonly do the same. For a sweeter sound, switch to major pentatonic built on the same root.

Choosing a scale for the chords

  • Song in a minor key: minor pentatonic or natural minor on the root chord.
  • Song in a major key: major pentatonic or major scale on the root chord.
  • A static minor vamp with a major IV chord (like Dm7 to G7): Dorian.
  • A major vamp with a flat VII chord (like E to D): Mixolydian.
  • 12-bar blues: minor pentatonic or blues scale from the key’s root throughout, shifting the target chord as the changes move.

Phrasing matters more than notes

Leave gaps. Repeat a short idea, then vary it. Ask, then answer. Bend or slide into the important notes, and end phrases on a chord tone with a longer note. A solo of five well-placed notes will beat a run of fifty with no shape.

Pentatonic boxes and CAGED shapes

Minor and major pentatonic share their notes

A minor pentatonic is A C D E G. C major pentatonic is C D E G A. These are the same five notes with a different home note. Pick A minor pentatonic, then press Relative: the key changes to C and the scale to major pentatonic, but the dots stay where they are. This is why a minor box sounds sad when you resolve to the minor root and happy when you resolve to the relative major root. Use the minor pentatonic over the minor chord, and the major pentatonic over the major chord three semitones up.

The five boxes

Each pentatonic scale has five shapes that tile the neck. The same five shapes cover both scales. Only the numbering and the root note change.

What CAGED is

CAGED is named after five open chords: C, A, G, E and D. Each of them can be moved up the neck with a barre and still be the same chord, and every chord shape has a scale shape wrapped around it. Joined end to end in the order C, A, G, E, D, they cover the whole neck for a given key and start again at the 12th fret. Learn the five shapes and you can find the chord, its arpeggio and the scale anywhere.

How to use CAGED mode

  1. Set Position system to CAGED shapes and choose a key and a scale.
  2. Pick one shape. The map shows the scale notes inside that shape, and the chord tones of the key’s home chord in teal. Those teal dots form the chord shape.
  3. Play the chord tones first, then the full scale around them.
  4. Move to the next shape up. Their ranges overlap by a fret or two, so you can slide from one to the other.
  5. Press Relative to see the same shapes as the other pentatonic, with the same fret ranges.

CAGED in minor keys and modes

CAGED shapes come from the parent major scale. A minor, Dorian on D and Mixolydian on E use the shapes of C major, C major and A major. The map works this out for you and says which major key the shapes follow. In minor keys, the teal dots show the minor tonic chord, so you still see the chord tones inside the shape.

Practice ideas

  • Choose one chord and play it in all five shapes, from low to high.
  • Play a shape’s scale, then only its chord tones, then a short melody that ends on the root.
  • Connect two neighbours by playing the scale up one string pair in the first shape and finishing the line in the second.
